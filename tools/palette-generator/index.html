    Palette Generator guide

    What each harmony is doing

    HarmonyColour-wheel relationshipBest used for
    ComplementaryBase hue and the hue 180° oppositeCalls to action and accents that must stand out
    AnalogousBase hue with neighbours about 30° either sideCalm, nature-like and editorial schemes
    TriadicThree hues spaced 120° apartPlayful, energetic designs with one clear dominant colour
    Split-complementaryBase hue plus the two hues beside its complementContrast that is easier to balance than a direct complement
    TetradicFour hues spaced 90° apartRich editorial/data palettes; choose one lead colour to avoid chaos
    MonochromaticOne hue at several lightness levelsInterfaces, states and depth without changing hue

    Turn swatches into a usable palette

    • Use the 60–30–10 heuristic: one dominant colour or neutral over most area, a secondary colour for support, and a small high-contrast accent. It is a starting balance, not a law.
    • Add near-black, white and greys deliberately. The generator focuses on hue relationships, but real interfaces spend most pixels on neutrals and text.
    • Assign jobs before polishing: background, surface, text, primary action, success/warning/error. A beautiful row of swatches can still fail if two colours compete for the same job.
    • View the palette at real sizes. A colour that looks rich in a large swatch may be unreadable as 12 px text or nearly invisible as a thin chart line.

    Accessibility is a pair-by-pair check

    Harmony describes hue relationships; accessibility depends mainly on luminance contrast between the two colours actually touching. Test body text, links, icons and focus indicators in the Color Contrast Checker, and do not communicate errors or selected states by colour alone. Darkening or lightening one palette step often fixes contrast while preserving the harmony.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which color harmony should I choose?

    Analogous colours sit next to each other on the wheel and feel calm and cohesive. Complementary colours sit opposite and create strong contrast. Triadic palettes are more vibrant but balanced, while split-complementary gives contrast with less tension. Monochromatic palettes vary lightness within one hue and are the safest starting point for interfaces.

    Are these palettes automatically accessible?

    No. A harmony can look good while text on a background still fails contrast requirements. Check every actual foreground/background pair with the Color Contrast Checker; normal text generally needs at least 4.5:1 for WCAG AA.

    How do I use the copied CSS variables?

    Paste the copied :root block into your stylesheet, then refer to the variables in other rules — for example, background: var(--triadic-1). Rename the variables to semantic names such as --brand or --accent if the palette becomes part of a design system.
