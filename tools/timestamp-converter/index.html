    Timestamp Converter guide

    Seconds vs milliseconds — the 1000× bug everyone ships once

    Feed a milliseconds value to code expecting seconds and your date lands in the year 55,000+; feed seconds to milliseconds-expecting code (like JavaScript’s new Date(x)) and everything piles up in January 1970. The digit count is the giveaway — 10 digits means seconds, 13 means milliseconds for any date near our era — and this converter applies exactly that heuristic (a trillion and above is read as milliseconds), then shows both forms so you can copy the one your system wants.

    One instant, many spellings

    The timestamp 1700000000 is the single instant 2023-11-14T22:13:20Z in ISO 8601 UTC. That same moment was already 15 November, 03:43 in India (UTC+5:30) while still 14 November afternoon in the Americas. A Unix timestamp itself has no timezone — it only acquires a calendar date when displayed through one, which is why the result above lists ISO (UTC), UTC and your local time side by side, plus a relative “x days ago” for logs.

    Landmarks worth knowing

    • 0 → 1970-01-01 00:00:00 UTC, the epoch itself.
    • 1,000,000,000 → 2001-09-09 01:46:40 UTC, the “billennium”.
    • 2,147,483,647 → 2038-01-19 03:14:07 UTC, the last second a signed 32-bit timestamp can hold.
    • Negative values work fine here: −1 is one second before the epoch, 1969-12-31 23:59:59 UTC.
    • Leap seconds are ignored by Unix time entirely — it pretends every day has exactly 86,400 seconds, which is why timestamp subtraction can differ from wall-clock subtraction across a leap-second insertion.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is my timestamp in seconds or milliseconds?

    Count the digits: current-era seconds have 10 digits (e.g. 1700000000), milliseconds have 13 (1700000000000). This converter auto-detects — values of a trillion or more are treated as milliseconds, everything smaller as seconds. JavaScript’s Date.now() gives milliseconds; most Unix tools and APIs (and PHP’s time()) give seconds.

    What date is timestamp 0?

    The Unix epoch: 1 January 1970, 00:00:00 UTC. Every Unix timestamp counts seconds (or milliseconds) elapsed since that instant, not counting leap seconds. Negative values simply count backwards into 1969 and earlier.

    What is the Year 2038 problem?

    Systems that store timestamps in a signed 32-bit integer overflow at 2,147,483,647 — which is 19 January 2038, 03:14:07 UTC — after which the count wraps to negative and dates jump to 1901. 64-bit systems are unaffected in any practical timeframe.
